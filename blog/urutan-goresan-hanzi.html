Stroke order in Chinese: the 8 rules that cover almost everything

2026-08-07 · Jeffrey Christian

Stroke order looks like bureaucracy until you understand what it is for. Nobody is watching you write, and a character drawn in the wrong order can still look correct on the page. So why does it matter?

Because order is how the character gets stored in your memory. A character written in a consistent order becomes a single motor sequence — one thing to recall — rather than a picture you have to reconstruct stroke by stroke every time. It is the difference between remembering a phone number as a rhythm and remembering eleven separate digits. Learners who skip stroke order can usually recognise characters and struggle to produce them, which is exactly the gap that shows up when you have to write something without a keyboard.

It also makes paper dictionaries usable, since stroke-count and radical lookup both depend on counting strokes the standard way — and it makes handwriting input more reliable, because those systems are built around the order most people write in.

The eight rules below handle most characters you will meet as a beginner. They are not exceptionless, and the exceptions are worth learning one at a time rather than trying to systematise.

1. Top to bottom. 三 is written as three horizontal strokes from the top down. This is the rule that overrides most others when they conflict.

2. Left to right. 川 goes left stroke, middle, right. Combined with the first rule, this handles a surprising amount on its own.

3. Horizontal before vertical when strokes cross. 十 is the horizontal first, then the vertical. So is 干, 士, and every character containing them.

4. Outside before inside. 月 starts with the enclosing shape before the two inner horizontals. 同, 用 and 周 all follow this.

5. Enclosures close last. 国 is written as the three sides of the box, then everything inside, then the bottom line seals it. Writing the full box first is the single most common beginner error.

6. Centre before wings in symmetrical characters. 小 is the central hook, then left, then right. 水 and 承 follow the same shape.

7. Left-falling before right-falling. 人 is the left-falling stroke then the right-falling one. This holds inside larger characters too — 文, 交, 这.

8. Dots and minor strokes last. The dot in 玉 comes after the 王 beneath it. Small finishing strokes usually close a character rather than open it.

The rules conflict sometimes, and when they do, top-to-bottom and left-to-right generally win. 里 is a good test: the 日 on top comes first as a complete unit, then the 土 beneath it, rather than trying to write all the horizontals together.

The fastest way to internalise any of this is to write the character while watching the order, not to memorise the list. Every character on this site has an animated stroke order you can replay, and a practice mode that tells you when the order or direction is wrong — which is the part a static diagram cannot do.
