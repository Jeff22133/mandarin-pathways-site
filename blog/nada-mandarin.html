Mandarin tones: the 4 tones, the neutral one, and why they feel hard

2026-08-07 · Jeffrey Christian

Mandarin has four tones and a neutral one. That much is in every textbook. What textbooks written for English speakers do not address is why tones are hard for a specific listener, and the answer differs depending on the language you already speak.

Indonesian is not tonal. Pitch in Indonesian does real work, but at the level of the sentence: it marks a question, or emphasis, or attitude. It never distinguishes one word from another. So the difficulty is not hearing pitch — Indonesian speakers hear pitch perfectly well — it is accepting that pitch belongs to the syllable rather than the sentence. 妈 (mā, mother) and 骂 (mà, to scold) are different words, not the same word said in two moods.

That single reframing is most of the work. The rest is knowing which pairs actually trip people up.

Second tone and third tone are the pair that causes the most trouble. Second tone rises. Third tone dips and then rises, and in ordinary speech the rise is often barely there — it mostly just sounds low. So they get confused in both directions: a third tone heard as a second, and a second produced as a third. If you drill one pair, drill this one.

First tone is high and level, and it feels unnatural to hold a pitch steady across a whole syllable when your first language lets pitch drift. It tends to sag toward the end. Holding it flat is a physical habit more than a listening skill.

Fourth tone falls sharply and often sounds angry or abrupt to Indonesian ears, because a sharp falling pitch in Indonesian carries exactly that. It is worth knowing this explicitly, because learners often soften their fourth tone to avoid sounding rude — and a softened fourth tone stops being a fourth tone.

The neutral tone is not a fifth tone; it is the absence of one. It takes its height from what came before, which is why it cannot be practised in isolation and only makes sense inside a word: 妈妈 (māma), 谢谢 (xièxie).

One rule you will meet early: when two third tones sit next to each other, the first becomes a second tone. 你好 is written nǐ hǎo and said ní hǎo. This is not an exception to memorise so much as something your mouth does anyway once you have said it enough times.

The practical approach is to drill tone pairs rather than single tones. A tone in isolation is easy; a tone next to another tone is where the real difficulty lives, and it is what actual speech consists of.
