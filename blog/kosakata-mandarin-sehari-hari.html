Everyday Mandarin vocabulary: the words you use before you can hold a conversation

2026-08-08 · Jeffrey Christian

Frequency lists are the usual way to pick a first vocabulary, and they are misleading. The most frequent words in Chinese are grammatical particles — 的, 了, 是 — which are essential to understanding a sentence and useless for producing one on your first day. What you need first is not the most common vocabulary but the most load-bearing: the words that let you complete an interaction rather than describe one.

There are roughly four situations that account for most early real-world Mandarin, and they are worth learning as situations rather than as a list.

Getting someone's attention and closing the exchange. 你好 to open, 请问 before a question to a stranger, 谢谢 and 不客气 to close, 对不起 and 没关系 when something goes wrong, 再见 to leave. These are formulaic — they never change form — which means each one is a complete tool the moment you learn it. That is unusual in language learning and worth exploiting early.

Buying things. 多少钱 is the single highest-return phrase in the language for a traveller, and 买单 gets you the bill in a restaurant. Numbers matter more than they look: prices are said quickly and you will understand nothing if the numbers are not automatic, so drill one to a hundred until they need no thought.

Saying what you want. 我要 plus a noun covers an enormous amount of ordering and asking — I want this, I want that. Pointing plus 这个 is not a failure of language, it is what everyone does, including native speakers in a noisy shop.

Admitting you are lost. 我听不懂 (I don't understand), 请说慢一点 (please speak more slowly), 可以 and 不用 for yes-ish and no-ish. Learners often skip these because they feel like an admission of weakness. They are the opposite: they are what lets a conversation continue instead of ending, and every hour you spend in a continuing conversation is worth more than an hour of study.

The useful discipline with everyday vocabulary is to learn it out loud and in context, never as a column of words with meanings beside them. A word you have only read is a word you will not produce under pressure — and everyday vocabulary exists entirely to be produced under pressure.
