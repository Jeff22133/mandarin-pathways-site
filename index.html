Questions

Is it free?+

Yes, and there are no ads. It may not be free forever — it is early and growing — but nothing is behind a payment today and anything you study now stays yours.

HSK 2.0 or HSK 3.0?+

Both. The app defaults to HSK 2.0 levels 1–6 because that is the standard the Standard Course textbooks teach, and HSK 3.0 bands 1–9 are there if you want the newer syllabus. You can switch in Settings.

Do I need to know any Chinese already?+

No. Lesson 1 begins at 你好 and assumes nothing — not the characters, not pinyin, not the tones. If you do know some, take the 5-minute placement and it will point you at the right lesson instead.

Does it actually teach handwriting?+

Yes, and it is the reason the app exists. 9,574 characters have full stroke data, and practice checks stroke order and direction, not just the finished shape.

Does it work on a phone?+

Yes. It runs in the browser, installs to the home screen, and keeps working offline. Handwriting practice is better with a finger on a phone than with a mouse.

Is there an Indonesian version?+

Yes — the whole interface, not a machine translation. Switch language in Settings. Word meanings can be set to Indonesian or English independently of the interface.

About learning Mandarin with HSK

HSK — Hànyǔ Shuǐpíng Kǎoshì — is the standard Chinese proficiency exam, and it is the most useful spine to hang self-study on. It fixes what to learn and in what order, which is the question that stalls most learners long before difficulty does. The older 2.0 standard runs from level 1 to 6; the 2021 revision, HSK 3.0, splits the same ground across nine bands and adds explicit handwriting requirements.

Which one you follow should depend on your materials. The HSK Standard Course series that most classes and self-studiers use is written to HSK 2.0, so if you are working from those books, level 1 there should mean level 1 in whatever app you use alongside them. That is why this one defaults to 2.0 rather than quietly renumbering your textbook.

The part learners underestimate is writing. Reading a character you have only ever seen is far easier than producing it from memory, and an app that only asks you to recognise will leave you fluent-feeling and stuck the moment you need to write. Practising stroke order also makes characters easier to remember, because it turns an arbitrary picture into a sequence with rules — which components appear, in what order, and where they sit.
