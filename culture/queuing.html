Queuing & public space

Formal queue lines — marked lanes at train stations, banks, and airports — are generally respected and enforced, especially in larger cities where queuing culture has strengthened noticeably over the past couple of decades through public campaigns. In less formally managed settings, like boarding a bus or squeezing into a crowded market stall, people commonly move up by proximity rather than strict arrival order, more so in some regions and among older crowds than others; if you notice this happening around you, moving forward calmly claims your spot rather than waiting to be invited.

Personal space in crowded public settings — subways, markets, sidewalks — tends to be smaller than many learners expect. Some jostling, close following, or squeezing past without an explicit 'excuse me' is normal and isn't meant as rude; a light 不好意思 still smooths things over if you're the one doing the moving.

On escalators, standing habits vary by city and have shifted over time — in some places standing on one side and walking on the other is common, in others people simply spread across both sides, especially when it's busy; watch what locals around you are doing rather than assuming one universal rule applies everywhere.

On public transit, offering your seat to elderly passengers, pregnant women, or parents with small children (让座) is a well-recognized courtesy, sometimes requested directly by a fellow passenger — 麻烦让一下座 — or simply gestured. Declining isn't inherently rude, but visibly ignoring a direct, polite request can draw quiet disapproval.

Useful phrases

  • 排队
    pái duì

    to queue / line up

  • 麻烦让一下
    má fan ràng yí xià

    excuse me, could you make way?

  • 不好意思
    bù hǎo yì si

    excuse me (moving past someone)

  • 您先请
    nín xiān qǐng

    after you (offering priority)

  • 这是最后一个人吗
    zhè shì zuì hòu yí ge rén ma

    is this the end of the line?

  • 给您让个座
    gěi nín ràng ge zuò

    let me give up my seat for you

Quick tips

  • •Formal lines at stations and airports are respected; looser settings may move by proximity rather than strict order — watch and match the room.
  • •A little jostling on crowded transit isn't rude; a light 不好意思 while moving through smooths it over.
  • •Offering your seat (让座) to elders, pregnant passengers, or parents with young kids is a well-recognized, appreciated courtesy.
